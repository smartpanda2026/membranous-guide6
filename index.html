    anti-PLA2R: serum vs. biopsy staining
    Serum anti-PLA2R antibody
    • Diagnostic: Positive in ~70–80% of primary MN. High titre (>50 RU/mL) with nephrotic proteinuria strongly supports primary MN — may permit noninvasive diagnosis in typical cases.
    • Disease activity: Titre tracks immunologic activity — rising titre predicts relapse; falling titre precedes clinical remission by months.
    • Prognostic: Persistently high or rising titre = higher risk of progression and lower chance of spontaneous remission.
    • Monitoring treatment: Serological remission (titre undetectable) is an early, sensitive marker of response to rituximab or CNI; guides re-dosing at 6 months.
    • Transplant: Pre-transplant titre predicts recurrent MN in the allograft.
    • Limitation: Can be negative early in disease or after immunosuppression — a negative serum test does not exclude PLA2R-associated MN.
    Renal biopsy PLA2R staining
    • Diagnosis: Granular IgG4-dominant deposits along the subepithelial side of the GBM with PLA2R co-localisation — the histologic hallmark of primary MN.
    • Discordance: Biopsy-positive but serum-negative can occur early or after immunosuppression; biopsy staining is more stable over time.
    • Immunologic remission: Loss of PLA2R staining on repeat biopsy correlates with sustained clinical remission.
    • Secondary vs. primary: PLA2R staining strongly favours primary MN. Absence prompts workup for secondary causes or other antigens.
    • Antigen panel: Full IF panel on biopsy tissue is required to identify non-PLA2R antigens — serum tests exist only for PLA2R and THSD7A.
    • Limitation: Staining is qualitative; serum titre is quantitative and better for serial monitoring.
    Clinical pearl: Use both together — serum titre for real-time monitoring and treatment decisions; biopsy staining for definitive diagnosis and antigen classification. Rising serum titre with stable biopsy staining = immunologic relapse before clinical relapse.
    Practical algorithm: Order serum anti-PLA2R at diagnosis → if positive and typical presentation, treat as PLA2R-associated MN. If negative, request full antigen IF panel on biopsy tissue. If all antigens negative → work up aggressively for secondary MN. Monitor serum anti-PLA2R at 3, 6, 12 months during treatment.

    Anticoagulation in nephrotic syndrome — when to start & stop
    Membranous nephropathy carries the highest thromboembolic risk of all nephrotic glomerular diseases
    Venous thromboembolism (VTE) often occurs within the first 6 months of diagnosis. Risk increases as serum albumin falls. The decision to anticoagulate is based on estimating thromboembolic risk vs. bleeding risk.
    WHEN TO START (prophylactic)
    • Serum albumin < 25 g/L — strongest single predictor; VTE risk rises sharply below this threshold
    • Serum albumin 25–30 g/L — consider if additional risk factors present (proteinuria > 8 g/d, BMI > 35, prior VTE, immobilization, genetic thrombophilia)
    • Proteinuria > 10 g/d — independent risk factor
    • First 6 months after diagnosis — highest-risk window
    • Use the online risk tool (UNC bleed risk calculator) to estimate patient-specific bleeding risk before starting
    • Decision rule: Anticoagulate when estimated VTE risk exceeds estimated bleeding risk
    WHEN TO STOP
    • After complete remission — proteinuria < 0.3 g/d and albumin normalized (> 35 g/L)
    • After partial remission with stable albumin — proteinuria < 3.5 g/d and albumin > 30 g/L for ≥ 3 months
    • If bleeding occurs — immediately stop, reverse if indicated, reassess risk-benefit
    • If a thromboembolic event occurred — anticoagulation is generally continued indefinitely (not stopped after remission)
    • Typical prophylactic duration — 3–6 months, or until remission of nephrotic syndrome (mean ~4 months in one study)

    DOACs in nephrotic syndrome — key limitation

    DOACs are heavily albumin-bound (apixaban 92–94%, rivaroxaban 92–95%) and are lost in urine when proteinuria is severe. The effects of hypoalbuminemia on drug dosing have not been studied. Despite favorable case reports, KDIGO 2021 and KDOQI commentary state these drugs cannot be generally recommended in nephrotic patients until pharmacokinetic studies are performed. Warfarin remains the anticoagulant of choice for confirmed VTE in MN.

    Side effects & monitoring
    Immediate / infusion reactions
    • Infusion reactions — 13% of patients; fever, chills, hypotension, bronchospasm, urticaria; usually occur 15–90 min after start
    • Anaphylaxis — 5.8% (rare but serious); have epinephrine, hydrocortisone, antihistamine ready
    • Management: Stop infusion, give IV antihistamine + hydrocortisone, restart at half rate if mild
    Delayed / long-term
    • Infection — most common AE; 40.6% overall in one cohort, 17.6% in pMN specifically. Respiratory infections most common (28.3%)
    • Hypogammaglobulinemia — 28.6% with long-term use; monitor IgG levels
    • Neutropenia — 5.5%; late-onset neutropenia can occur weeks after infusion
    • Interstitial lung disease / pneumonitis — rare (0.1%) but serious; consider if new hypoxia/dyspnea
    • Hepatitis B reactivation — screen all patients (HBsAg, anti-HBc) before treatment
    Elderly patients (≥ 60 years) — special caution
    • ▸ Higher risk of infection due to age-related immune senescence + B-cell depletion + hypoalbuminemia
    • ▸ Low-dose, long-course regimen preferred — e.g., 375 mg/m² × 4 weekly doses rather than 1g × 2
    • ▸ Eosinophil percentage drop at 1 month may predict infection risk better than CD19+ B-cell count
    • ▸ Monitor closely for infection in the first 3–6 months after RTX
    Rituximab non-responder · stepwise algorithm
    Defined as: no proteinuria reduction > 25% at 6 months AND anti-PLA2R not falling OR rising
    1
    Confirm non-response & exclude mimics

    Verify adherence, rule out infection, malignancy, NSAIDs, or superimposed diabetic nephropathy. Re-check anti-PLA2R, proteinuria, eGFR.

    → Re-biopsy if atypical course
    2
    Optimize rituximab exposure

    Check CD19/CD20 count. If B-cells repopulated, re-dose rituximab 1g × 2 (days 0, 14). Consider higher dose if incomplete depletion.

    → Re-dose RTX 1g × 2
    3
    Switch or combine mechanism

    Add CNI (tacrolimus 0.05–0.1 mg/kg/d) OR switch to cyclophosphamide + alternate-month steroids (Ponticelli).

    → TAC + RTX or CYC protocol
    4
    Third-line / rescue therapy

    If high risk & refractory: obinutuzumab (anti-CD20), daratumumab (anti-CD38), belimumab + RTX, or ACTH gel. Consider clinical trial enrollment.

    → Obinutuzumab / anti-CD38 / trial
    ⚠️ Important: Before declaring rituximab failure, exclude secondary MN (malignancy, infection, drug) and confirm adequate B-cell depletion. Rising anti-PLA2R with stable proteinuria may be early immunologic relapse — re-treat with RTX before proteinuria worsens.

    Prednisone in MN · Evidence summary
    ❌ Prednisone monotherapy is NOT recommended

    Cochrane 2014 + KDIGO 2021/2025: corticosteroids alone do NOT increase remission rates.
